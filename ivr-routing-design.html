IVR Call Routing — Proposed Solution

A meaningful share of CSP ↔ customer calls drop today. This is the design that closes the gap.

The problem (in brief)

A meaningful share of CSP ↔ customer calls fail to connect today. The current design only works when the User (CSP / Customer) taps the in-app Call CTA from a recognised SIM and dials immediately — every other initiation pattern (callbacks, dialer redials, calls from unregistered SIMs, multi-ticket customers, colleague forwarding) drops silently.

Constraints — what makes this hard

The conditions of today's call surface the design has to handle:

  • Caller may not be on their registered number. They may be on a secondary SIM, a family phone, or an unregistered device — we can't always recognise them by their FROM.
  • A CSP can have many active customers at once. Knowing it's the CSP doesn't tell us which customer they want to reach.
  • Multiple humans may share one CSP App login. A technician (e.g., Rohit) signing into the Owner's CSP App is the canonical case — the dialing identity and the logged-in identity don't match.
  • A customer may have multiple active tickets simultaneously. Restore + Pickup can co-exist; the system needs to disambiguate which technician they want to reach.
  • The customer may call back from their dialer with no context. They've seen a missed call on a masked number and dial back — in their mental model, calling back "just works." It doesn't today.
  • A CSP may forward the call to a colleague. Common informal field-ops practice; the colleague has no Wiom app installed and is on a different phone.
  • The persona is voice-first and non-app-engaged. A graceful fallback can't rely on opening an app or reading a screen mid-call.

For the full breakdown of today's two-masked-number mechanism and the specific failure modes it produces, see Archives at the bottom.

Objective & framing

At its core, this spec describes an authentication system. Every routing decision is an identity resolution; the tables are the identity store; the PIN is the credential. Failure modes are best understood through this lens — anything that fails is an identity-resolution failure, and the question is whether the failure is graceful or silent.

Four terms used throughout

TermMeaning
Recognised The caller's identity can be resolved — their number is in an active mapping (Table 1), or they hold a valid PIN (Table 2).
Authorised The connection is legitimate — the PIN entered (or the FROM matched) belongs to an active ticket for the party being reached.
Seamless The call connects without the user opening an app, navigating UI, or understanding masked numbers. For the in-app CTA path: zero friction. For the fallback path: friction survivable by a voice-first, non-app field user.
Graceful fallback For unrecognised or unauthorised callers — the user understands why the call did not connect and has a clear, achievable next step. Not a dead-end.

The proposed solution

One bidirectional masked number serves both call directions, backed by a small lookup engine that always tries to recognise the caller. When the caller isn't recognised from their number alone, an authorisation step (below) takes over — and on terminal failure a graceful fallback tells the user what to do next.

Three pillars

  1. One bidirectional masked number. Serves both call directions; preserves the seamless in-app dial; opens up off-app initiation patterns (callbacks, dialer redials, calls from new SIMs, multi-ticket cases, colleague forwarding).
  2. Recognise-then-route. When Table 1 misses, the user_identification API tells us if the caller is a known customer / CSP user and how many active counterparties they have. One counterparty → bridge directly, no PIN. Multiple counterparties or unknown caller → PIN authorises and disambiguates. See Authorization resolution below.
  3. Two small tables. Table 1 for the active call mapping (today's behaviour, kept) and Table 2 for per-ticket credentials (new).

Calling eligibility

Calls in either direction are enabled only when the customer has an active ticket of one of these types:

  • Install — onboarding ticket (booking → installed)
  • Restore — service-restoration ticket (recovery after outage / issue)
  • Pickup — equipment / router pickup ticket

When no active ticket exists for a customer, no Table 1 mapping is written, no PIN is issued. Any inbound dial in that state lands in the dead-end IVR.

Authorization resolution

The problem this solves

Once Table 1 doesn't tell us where to bridge a call, we need to figure out the right counterparty. Two flavours of "don't know where to bridge":

  • The caller is unknown — their FROM doesn't match any customer or CSP we recognise. We can't route them anywhere safely without further proof of who they are or who they want to reach.
  • The caller is known, but ambiguous — we recognise them (customer or CSP user), but they have multiple active tickets. We don't know which counterparty they want to reach.

Authorization resolution closes both gaps using one mechanism: identify what we can, then ask the caller for a PIN only when identification alone isn't enough.

How it works

When Table 1 misses, the system calls user_identification(FROM). The response carries three things: the user type (customer / csp / unknown), the number of active tickets, and — if exactly one ticket — the counterparty's mobile (fetched from Table 2 by ticket_id + side; no separate table needed).

  • Recognised user, 1 active ticket → routing is unambiguous → bridge directly to the counterparty. No PIN.
  • Recognised user, 2 or more active tickets → known caller but ambiguous routing → IVR asks for a 5-digit PIN; Table 2 looks up PIN → other_party_mobile. The PIN itself identifies the ticket.
  • Unknown FROM → caller might still hold a valid PIN (e.g., UC 13 colleague forwarding) → IVR asks for the PIN. If valid → bridge; if 3 wrong → dead-end.
  • Recognised user, 0 active tickets (e.g., closed-ticket customer dialling back) → no PIN exists for them in Table 2, so we skip the prompt entirely → dead-end IVR. The user type chooses which helpline message plays.

Where the PIN comes from: the customer receives theirs via SMS / WhatsApp at ticket creation (along with the masked number to dial); the CSP sees theirs on the ticket card in the CSP App. For colleague forwarding (UC 13), the CSP forwards the same SMS — it carries everything the colleague needs.

Guardrails: 3-retry cap per call; PIN is immutable for the ticket's lifetime (no rotation — both parties already hold the PIN, rotating would force re-distribution); PIN value is held in a 90-day cooldown after ticket close to prevent stale-PIN cross-pollination on reuse; for recognised CSP callers, Table 2 lookups are scoped to that CSP's active tickets.

Assumptions

  • CSP and Customer cannot share the same registered mobile (Wiom's data model rejects dual-role).
  • Calling is disabled once a ticket is closed; no calls outside active Install / Restore / Pickup tickets.
  • No guest access — an unrecognised caller without a valid PIN cannot connect.
  • A valid PIN equals authorisation; we do not verify identity beyond PIN possession. A leaked or shared PIN bypasses authorisation.
  • PIN possession is sufficient regardless of who holds it — colleague forwarding (UC04) is an explicit supported case.
  • PIN delivery is reliable — customer's SMS / WhatsApp reaches them before the call is needed; CSP's ticket card is current.
  • IVR / PIN resolution runs without noticeable delay — backend lookups < 200 ms p95 within Exotel's 5-second budget.

Missed-call notifications

Successfully dialling the recipient does not mean the call was picked. Field CSPs are often on rooftops, in cable ducts, or low-signal areas; customers are busy, away, or screening. A missed call without a follow-up is a silent failure — and silent failures erode trust fast.

We close this gap by ingesting Exotel's call-disposition webhook and triggering a CleverTap campaign:

  1. Exotel disposition webhook — Exotel fires a callback for every call with the outcome (answered / no-answer / busy / failed). We listen on this webhook.
  2. Backend → CleverTap event — On any non-answered outcome, the backend fires a typed CleverTap event (e.g. missed_call_CSP_to_customer or missed_call_customer_to_CSP) with the parties involved.
  3. CleverTap campaign → notification — The campaign sends an SMS / push to the party who missed the call:
    • Customer missed → notify customer ("Your technician tried to reach you. Call back on [number]").
    • CSP missed → notify CSP ("Your customer tried to reach you. Call back on [number]").

The missed-call alert is also the natural PIN reminder. When the missed party calls back, the easy case is their FROM is recognised and they have a single active ticket → bridge directly, no PIN (Paths 2). When PIN is needed (multi-ticket caller or callback from an unregistered SIM), the alert message points them to where their PIN lives: customer → the SMS / WhatsApp received at ticket creation (in their inbox); CSP → the ticket card in the CSP App. This keeps PIN discovery zero-friction for the callback flow.

Dead-end SMS (recipient = the caller who hit the dead-end). When the IVR reaches a dead-end state (no active tech / PIN exhausted), an SMS is also sent to the caller's number with the relevant helpline (call-centre or trust line) — so the caller has the number in writing once the call ends. This is in addition to the voice message, not a replacement.

IVR language: Hindi. All IVR prompts (PIN entry, dead-end messages, any future menus) and all outbound notification copy default to Hindi, in line with Wiom's voice-first Bharat user. Regional-language expansion based on CSP geography will follow. Exact wording — for every IVR prompt, SMS template, missed-call alert, and notification — will be decided by the solutions team.
Comms layer dependencies (must land before launch). All outbound SMS — PIN delivery at ticket open, missed-call alerts, dead-end SMS — must be sent via DLT-registered templates with the "Wiom" sender ID, so recipients see "Wiom" as the sender rather than a generic 11444XXXX phone number. WhatsApp comms require the corresponding WhatsApp BSP setup with approved templates.

Building trust for the masked number

Routing the call is only half the job — the recipient still has to pick up. Two whitelisting layers are in place so the masked number shows as a recognised Wiom caller rather than an unknown or "suspected spam" number. Together they directly attack the DNP (Did-Not-Pick) issue:

  • Truecaller whitelisting — the masked number shows with a green verified banner and the Wiom name in Truecaller (which the majority of Bharat users have installed).
  • VLT whitelisting — telco-level verified-logo trust mark; the number is flagged at the network layer as a legitimate business caller.

The two layers are independent and complementary — Truecaller covers app-side identification, VLT covers network-side identification. Both build trust that the call is legitimate, not spam.

Edge cases (terminal states, not standalone use cases). A caller with 0 active tickets — a customer whose ticket has closed, or a CSP with no current assignments — lands at the graceful-fallback IVR straight from user_identification, skipping the PIN prompt entirely. The same IVR plays after 3 wrong PIN attempts on any path. Message branches by user type: customer → call centre 88803 22222; CSP / unknown → trust line 78368 11111.

Metrics

Top-line metrics for tracking this build's impact:

  • Connect rate — share of dialled calls that reach the recipient's phone ringing.
  • Direct-connect hit rate — share of calls that resolve via Table 1 (no PIN needed).
  • PIN entry success rate — share of PIN prompts where the caller enters a valid PIN within 3 attempts.
  • PIN abandonment rate — share of PIN prompts where the caller hangs up without entering anything.
  • Missed-call recovery rate — share of missed calls where the missed party calls back (within 24 h).
  • Dead-end exit rate — share of calls that end at the call-centre or trust-line IVR; split by which exit was played.
  • CSP-side vs Customer-side PIN usage — split of which side enters PIN, to size demand and abuse signals.
  • Recipient pickup rate — share of bridged calls that are answered; tracked against Truecaller-recognised vs not.

Archives

How masking works today, and the use-case gap the proposed solution closes.

How it works today — MN1 / MN2

The current implementation uses two separate masked numbers per call session, one per direction:

MN1
Customer → MN1 → CSP
One-way route. Only customer-initiated calls work.
MN2
CSP → MN2 → Customer
One-way route. Only CSP-initiated calls work.

Each is minted via initiateCall when the originating side taps the Call CTA in their app, and bound to a short-lived FROM → TO mapping in the backend. MN1 only knows how to route Customer → CSP; MN2 only the reverse.
